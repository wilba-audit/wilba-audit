# Treat Other Videos as Leads, Not Footage

The short answer for Jess: watching other creators' videos to *find* GTA 6 code claims is fine and normal practice, but taking their footage and "repurposing" it with Higgsfield or Artlist is not. Every rule that matters works against it: YouTube's reused-content policy (which can demonetise the whole channel), copyright, Australia's narrow fair dealing exceptions, TikTok and Instagram originality rules, and both tools' own licence terms. As of 18 Nov 2026, the day before GTA VI launches on PS5 and Xbox Series X|S, **no official source has confirmed a single GTA 6 cheat code**. The only legitimate "offers" are two pre-order bonuses, the Vintage Vice City Pack and one month of GTA+, and they are bonuses rather than codes you type in. The strongest new lead is a set of major-outlet cheat lists (GameSpot, Game Rant, Insider Gaming) that appeared on review-embargo day. We could only see these as search snippets, so they are logged as Unverified claim-001 until Jess reads them and tests each input on her own console. The channel's edge is speed with proof: spot a claim anywhere, credit the source, test it on camera, and cut her *own* footage into Shorts, Reels and TikToks. Higgsfield and Artlist are useful for music, sound effects, branding and reformatting her own clips, not for remixing anyone else's video.

## The "repurpose other creators" playbook breaks three platforms' rules at once

The playbook Jess saw comes in two versions. The "safe" version, now pushed by the tool vendors themselves, copies the *structure* of a successful video and generates new footage. Higgsfield's own blog teaches exactly that: take a proven video's format, script it, generate it ([Higgsfield blog](https://higgsfield.ai/blog/faceless-channel)). Its income example ("$39,500 a month") is a vendor claim with no verification behind it. The risky version reuploads, clips or lightly reworks other creators' footage, sometimes with AI voice-over or upscaling on top ([HowToAI digest](https://recapio.com/digest/how-i-make-monetizable-shorts-by-just-copying-peoples-content-by-howtoai)). Even SEO and creator-tool blogs now warn that this second version is the one getting channels demonetised ([OutlierKit](https://outlierkit.com/resources/faceless-youtube-channel-demonetized/)).

**YouTube** enforces this on three separate tracks. On *monetisation*, the Shorts and YouTube Partner Program policies name "reuploading other creators' content from YouTube or another platform" and "compilations with no original content added" as non-original. They also say monetisation "may be removed from your entire channel" if YouTube "cannot clearly tell that you made the content". Clips used for a critical review are allowed ([YouTube Help: Shorts monetization](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/12504220?hl=en); [YouTube Help: YPP policies](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1311392?hl=en)). On *reach*, YouTube said in October 2026 it would "reduce the reach of content re-uploaded from other creators without adding anything of your own". Shorts cut from your own long-form are exempt ([Search Engine Journal](https://www.searchenginejournal.com/youtube-original-shorts-reposted-clips-reach/591774/)). On *copyright*, three strikes within 90 days terminates the channel and any linked channels ([Wikipedia summary of YouTube policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YouTube_copyright_strike)). The "inauthentic content" rule (renamed July 2025, with clearer guidance in July 2026) also targets mass-produced, templated AI video ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/07/09/youtube-prepares-crackdown-on-mass-produced-and-repetitive-videos-as-concern-over-ai-slop-grows); [Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2026/07/13/youtube-inauthentic-content-monetization-policy-update/)). We could not read the three categories in the July 2026 update.

**TikTok** and **Instagram** apply the same logic. TikTok's Creator Rewards treats content "reproduced from others with only slight modifications" as unoriginal. Repeat offences can make a whole account ineligible for the For You feed ([FindClout](https://findclout.com/blog/tiktok-creator-rewards-reposted-clips); [Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/tiktok-is-trying-to-clean-up-its-for-you-recommendations-210057825.html)). Instagram swaps reposted Reels for the original in recommendations ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/instagrams-algorithm-overhaul-will-reward-original-content-and-penalize-aggregators-130018977.html)). In April 2026 it stopped recommending accounts that mainly share content they didn't create or meaningfully transform to non-followers, across Reels, photos and carousels ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2026/04/30/new-instagram-policies-target-reposted-content/)). Visible watermarks from other apps are a red flag on both.

### Copyright protects their footage, not the code, and Australia is stricter than the US

The law draws a clean line here. **A cheat code is a fact, and nobody owns facts.** The US Supreme Court in *Feist* (1991) held that only original expression is protected, "not … the facts themselves" ([Feist, 499 U.S. 340](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USREPORTS-499/pdf/USREPORTS-499-340.pdf)). Another creator's footage, voice-over, script, edit and thumbnail *are* protected. US fair use is a four-factor, case-by-case test in which transformation matters most. YouTube states plainly that "giving credit to the owner of a copyrighted work won't by itself turn a non-transformative copy of their material into fair use", and writing "no infringement intended" doesn't help either ([YouTube Help: Fair use](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/9783148?hl=en); [YouTube Help: Fair use FAQ](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/6396261)). Jess is in Australia, which has **no general fair use**. The Copyright Act 1968 allows fair dealing only for listed purposes: research or study, criticism or review, parody or satire, reporting news, and professional advice. The use must also be "fair", and criticism or review and news reporting require sufficient acknowledgement ([ALRC](https://www.alrc.gov.au/publication/copyright-and-the-digital-economy-dp-79/7-fair-dealing/current-law/)). The ALRC recommended US-style fair use in 2013, but we found no sign it was ever enacted ([ALRC Report 122](https://www.alrc.gov.au/publication/copyright-and-the-digital-economy-alrc-report-122/4-the-case-for-fair-use/fair-use-builds-on-australias-fair-dealing-tradition/)). The practical result: learning "code X exists" from someone's video and testing it yourself is clean. Showing two seconds of a *fake-code* video while debunking it, with credit, may fit "criticism or review", but that is a judgement call. Using their clips as filler fits no exception.

## Higgsfield and Artlist are for making your own assets, not laundering others'

Neither tool's licence gives Jess rights she doesn't already hold. **Higgsfield's** help centre says you own your outputs and can use them commercially on any plan, YouTube monetisation included ([Higgsfield Help](https://higgsfield.ai/creator-hub/help-center/account/who-owns-my-generations-and-can-i-use-them-commercially)). Under the indemnity clause, *you* cover Higgsfield's losses if they do. Higgsfield only offers IP protection to Enterprise customers ([Aliteq summary](https://aliteq.com/higgsfield-commercial-use)). Its contest rules go further and ban unauthorised "characters, franchises, brands, logos" ([Higgsfield contest rules](https://higgsfield.ai/contests/talent-contest?tab=rules)). A July 2026 critique says the licence Higgsfield takes over user uploads is "perpetual, worldwide … sublicensable" ([StartupFortune](https://startupfortune.com/higgsfield-ai-tells-creators-they-own-their-videos-but-quietly-claims-a-perpetual-worldwide-license-to-train-on-them/)). Higgsfield's own pages say otherwise, and we couldn't read the Terms to settle it. Until it is settled, don't upload unreleased evidence footage. The Higgsfield connector in this workspace does include a "import media from a URL" tool. That makes remixing technically *possible*, but it grants no rights. **Artlist** licenses music, sound effects, stock footage and AI outputs for YouTube monetisation while you are subscribed. You register your channel on its **Clearlist** to avoid Content ID claims ([Artlist Help: Clearlist](https://help.artlist.io/hc/en-us/articles/29648622402333-Understanding-Artlist-s-Clearlist)). Videos published during the subscription stay cleared after you cancel; anything published after cancelling is not ([Artlist Help](https://help.artlist.io/hc/en-us/articles/29490991524253)). Its business terms forbid inputs or outputs that infringe third-party IP and disclaim liability for them ([Artlist Business terms](https://artlist.io/help-center/privacy-terms/business-package/)). The Social tier covers your own channels only. Client work needs Pro or Business. An Artlist track also does nothing for GTA radio songs playing underneath it, and those are a known source of Content ID claims ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/get-full-youtube-monetization-gaming-videos/)).

Remixing Rockstar footage is a separate question that neither tool can answer. Rockstar's video policy, as summarised in search results, allows fan gameplay for "non-commercial" use. It defines that as not making "money through the game footage you post". It removes isolated cutscenes, endings and pre-release or leaked footage ([Rockstar Support](https://support.rockstargames.com/articles/200153756/Policy-on-posting-copyrighted-Rockstar-Games-material)). Monetised GTA channels are widely *tolerated* but not licensed ([Prepublish, secondary](https://prepublish.ai/blog/gta-6-youtube-copyright-monetization)), which is why the plan's step of emailing Take-Two before monetising still matters. AI-generated GTA characters, logos or "leak-looking" scenes are precisely what Take-Two is pursuing. Realistic AI scenes also need disclosure on YouTube, and since May 2026 YouTube auto-labels undisclosed AI content ([YouTube Help: altered content](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491); [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/youtube-will-now-automatically-detect-and-label-ai-videos/)). TikTok does the same ([TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/tiktok-ssa-shares-more-ways-to-spot-shape-and-understand-ai-generated-content?lang=en-ZA)). AI "proof" footage would also break the venture's own core rule.

## A faster compliant loop: lead in, test on camera, cut your own clips

The legal route can also be the fast one. A PS5 can save up to the last 60 minutes of gameplay from a background buffer, set under Settings > Captures and Broadcasts ([PlayStation Support](https://www.playstation.com/en-us/support/games/capture-ps5-gameplay-screenshots)). So the moment a code works, one press of the Create button produces original proof footage that serves as both the ledger evidence and the Short. The loop runs like this. The monitoring stack flags a claim. It gets logged as Unverified with the source link, timestamp and poster's handle. Jess tests it on her console and saves the clip. She sets the status. The verdict Short goes out within hours, and Jess's own VOD or long-form goes through CapCut, OpusClip or Descript for more clip candidates ([Castmagic](https://www.castmagic.io/blog/opus-clip-pricing)). Credit follows the norms that serious outlets publish. Insider Gaming credits original sources ([Insider Gaming](https://insider-gaming.com/?p=157440)). Pure Xbox says "if we are unable to corroborate relevant information, we will always state this clearly" ([Pure Xbox](https://www.purexbox.com/fact-checking)). IGN corrects by dated update rather than deleting ([IGN corrections](https://ign-entertainment.squarespace.com/corrections-policy)). On screen, that becomes a text lower-third ("Claim: [effect], first seen from @Creator, [date]"), a link and timestamp in the description, and then *only Jess's footage*. Credit is a courtesy that builds trust. It is not a licence. Formats such as "Myth vs Tested", "Viewer-submitted codes tested" and "Scam breakdowns" add the original voice and analysis that the reused-content policy rewards.

The monitoring stack can be almost free. F5Bot sends Reddit keyword alerts by email, free with a delay on the free tier ([F5Bot](https://f5bot.com/)). X advanced search can filter for recent, liked posts, for example `"GTA 6" (cheat OR code) -filter:replies min_faves:50 since:<today>` ([Typefully](https://typefully.com/blog/twitter-x-advanced-search)). The rest are YouTube and TikTok search sorted by newest and checked hourly in launch week, Reddit /new RSS feeds for r/GTA6 and r/GTA, Google Alerts, and for offers the official sources only: Rockstar Newswire and the PlayStation, Xbox and Rockstar Store pages. Community sources are leads for *cheat inputs*. Promo-code claims from the community overlap heavily with scams. Two production rules matter on day one. Turn down in-game radio and music during capture, because Rockstar has announced no streamer mode and licensed songs trigger Content ID ([Eklipse, secondary](https://blog.eklipse.gg/streaming-tips/gta-6-music-radio.html)). Keep graphic violence out of the first ~7–8 seconds and out of thumbnails, under YouTube's September 2026 ad rules ([Dexerto](https://www.dexerto.com/gta/youtube-updates-gaming-violence-monetization-rules-ahead-of-gta-6-launch-3405945/)). Outlets disagree on whether the cutoff is 7 or 8 seconds.

## Conclusion

The guru pitch blurs together two things the law and the platforms treat very differently: *learning a fact* from another video, and *taking the footage*. For a codes channel, almost all the value is in the fact, which is free to use, while all the risk sits in the footage, which can cost monetisation on the whole channel, bring copyright strikes and leave posts invisible on Instagram and TikTok. Jess's plan already says "only our own captures". The missing piece is an explicit, written leads-only rule and a sourcing workflow that makes crediting and testing faster than copying.

Launch timing favours a trust-first approach.
